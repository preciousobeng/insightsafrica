How it works

We never talk to a satellite.
Nobody needs to.

The most common question we get — from students, researchers and journalists — is "how do you connect to the satellites, especially with poor network in rural areas?" The answer surprises almost everyone: there is no dish, no ground station, and no signal between us and space. Here is the entire chain, honestly drawn.

the whole chain — every link is public infrastructure or open data

The part that matters for Africa: a satellite measures rain over a village whether or not that village has ever had a network signal. Remote sensing works precisely where infrastructure doesn't. Connectivity only becomes a question when a person wants to view the data — which is why this site is lightweight and free.

The five steps, in plain language

Satellites photograph clouds — for their own reasons

Space agencies (NASA, NOAA, EUMETSAT) run weather satellites that constantly image Africa in infrared. Very cold cloud tops are a strong hint of rainfall. The agencies downlink this imagery to their own ground stations and publish it. We are not involved in any of this.

Rain gauges measure the truth on the ground

Satellites estimate; gauges know. National meteorological agencies across the continent maintain physical rain gauges. Their readings are the "ground" part of the story: not receivers or antennas, just honest buckets.

CHIRPS blends the two and gives it away

The Climate Hazards Center at UC Santa Barbara combines the satellite estimates with the gauge readings into one calibrated dataset: CHIRPS — rainfall for every ~5 km square of Africa, every month since 1981, published free on the internet. The same source NASA and the World Bank use.

Our pipeline turns pixels into districts

Each month our server downloads the newest CHIRPS file — the same way you download a PDF — and computes statistics for every district: rainfall totals, the 1991–2020 long-term average, and how far this month deviates from normal. That's the intelligence layer, and the code is open source.

You see answers, not raster files

Maps, district profiles and a free API — deliberately lightweight so it loads on a modest connection anywhere. No paywall, no login.

Questions students actually ask us

Do you communicate with the satellites directly?

No — and neither do most people who work with satellite data. The space agencies operate the satellites and publish the data; the entire earth-observation ecosystem downstream of them works from those public archives. You could start doing the same today.

Doesn't poor rural network coverage break this?

No, because nothing is transmitted from rural Ghana. The measurement happens from orbit and the gauge data travels through the met agencies' own channels. A village with zero connectivity is measured exactly as well as central Accra.

How accurate is satellite rainfall?

Good, not perfect. Infrared estimates can misjudge certain storm types, which is exactly why CHIRPS blends in gauge readings. For district-level monthly patterns — our use case — it is a well-validated, peer-reviewed standard. We also publish our methodology openly so you can judge for yourself.

Can I use this data for my research or coursework?

Yes, please. The maps and the API are free to use with attribution to InsightsAfrica, and CHIRPS itself is freely available if you want to work with the raw data. If you're a student building on this, we'd genuinely like to hear about it — write to us via the contact form.

Want to go deeper?

The raw dataset: CHIRPS at the UCSB Climate Hazards Center. Our full methodology write-up and the open-source pipeline are linked from the Ghana hub. And if you're a researcher or student who wants to collaborate, that's exactly what this platform is for — get in touch.
